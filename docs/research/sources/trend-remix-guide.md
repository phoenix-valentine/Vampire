## What it is

The last piece of the owner's toolkit [p. 1]. Recipe: **one classic + one twist + one trend**, checked against
what makes games great, then built with Claude using the roadmap [p. 1]. Parts 1 and 2 are trend research; the
Top trends list is the shortlist to actually use; the combination method and worked examples show the recipe;
the idea filter stops you chasing a trend that dies before you ship [p. 1]. Checked October 23, 2026; refresh
every three to six months; trends marked *lasting* outlive fads [p. 1]. It is written for small browser games
[p. 4]. It has nothing on touch controls, layout, performance, audio, difficulty curves or balance.

## Frameworks, lists and checklists

### F1. What the charts say (hard data) [p. 1-2]

Framing: small, original games are having their best year in a long time; the biggest small hits are cheap,
simple to grasp, and built to be shared [p. 1].

- **Indie and new games broke out more than ever.** Sensor Tower 2026 cohort (Sept 2025 to Aug 2026): games
  selling 500,000+ copies in their first month rose from 55 to 76; indie breakouts from 13 to 33; indie share
  of first-month sales from 26% to 38% [p. 1].
- **"Friendslop" led indie sales.** Cheap online co-op and party games (MECCHA CHAMELEON, How to Fish, RV There
  Yet?) were 46% of indie first-month sales. In 2025 R.E.P.O., PEAK and Schedule I together sold about 42
  million units [p. 1].
- **Solo-made survivors-likes still win.** Megabonk, "a 3D take on Vampire Survivors by one anonymous
  developer", sold a million copies in two weeks and peaked at 117,336 players on Steam [p. 1].
- **Roguelike deckbuilders keep selling.** Slay the Spire 2 sold 5.9 million in its first month (Sensor Tower);
  Balatro passed 5 million [p. 1].
- **Mobile downloads are ruled by simple puzzles.** Block Blast! was the most downloaded mobile game of 2025
  and reports 70 million daily players; in August 2026 it topped both app stores while arrow and traffic
  puzzles (Arrows-Puzzle Escape, Bus Traffic Fever!) filled the download charts [p. 1-2].
- **Mobile money stays with old giants.** Revenue charts are led by long-running match-3, merge, strategy and
  MOBA games. Hyper-casual hits enter and leave the charts quickly [p. 2].
- **Kids live on Roblox.** Steal a Brainrot hit 25.4 million players at once in October 2025 with a simple
  collect-and-steal loop spread through TikTok and YouTube clips [p. 2].
- **"Cozy" is Steam's biggest growing keyword.** Among games earning $100,000+, "cozy" rose 675% from 2022 to
  2025 (GameDiscoverCo); "solo" +450%; "factory" +325% [p. 2].
- **Browser games on itch.io:** simulation and strategy lead the popular web games list (a snapshot); idle and
  incremental tags are prominent [p. 2].

### F2. What they have in common (five traits of the hits) [p. 2]

1. **Instantly readable.** Understood from a five-second clip (Block Blast, Megabonk, Steal a Brainrot).
2. **Cheap or free.** Most breakout indie hits cost under $10.
3. **Clip-friendly.** Funny failures, huge combos and chaos make videos that sell the game.
4. **Short sessions with long pull.** Runs of minutes, inside a bigger loop of unlocks and collections.
5. **Remixes of proven formulas.** Survivors-likes, deckbuilders and block puzzles all build on known games.

### F3. What players say they're tired of (opinion and reporting) [p. 2]

- **AI slop.** At the October 2026 Steam Next Fest players complained about a flood of low-effort AI-made demos
  and called for an AI filter. "Your game must look and feel handmade and cared for."
- **Live-service chores.** Grinds, battle passes, fear of missing out; players favour complete games they can
  finish at their own pace.
- **Franchise fatigue.** Players want genuinely new experiences, which suits original small games.

### F4. Wider culture: what the data says [p. 3]

Things break out when people can join in, not just watch. YouTube reaches 75% of US children aged 2-12 and 80%
of 10-12 year olds. In YouTube's 2026 Culture & Trends research, things break through when people can take
part (46%) and understand them (41%). The Backrooms passed 15 billion views and became a film. 71% of 14-44
year olds have watched videos unpacking a fandom; 82% of 14-24 year olds turn to YouTube for fandom content.
61% of 14-24 year old fans like independent YouTube animated series as much as or more than studio shows.

### F6. Top trends to build on: fifteen, "roughly in order of usefulness" [p. 4-5]

Rule: *lasting* trends are safe to build a game around; *fads* are best used as flavour [p. 4].

1. **Roguelike runs with upgrade choices** (lasting). Evidence: Megabonk, Slay the Spire 2, Balatro. Appeals
   to: teens and adults. Use it: short runs with a pick-one-of-three upgrade after each wave [p. 4].
2. **Survivors-likes** (lasting). Evidence: Megabonk's million copies in two weeks. Appeals to: everyone who
   wants power without hard controls. Use it: auto-attack, move to dodge, screen fills with enemies and
   effects [p. 4].
3. **Drag-and-clear block and arrow puzzles** (lasting). Evidence: Block Blast!'s 70 million daily players;
   arrow puzzles in the August top 15. Appeals to: adults on short breaks. Use it: a calm puzzle you can play
   in one minute, with satisfying clears [p. 4].
4. **Cozy, low-stress play** (lasting). Evidence: +675% "cozy" among $100K+ Steam games. Appeals to: adults and
   players who want to relax. Use it: no fail state, gentle goals, plants, pets, shops [p. 4].
5. **Creature collecting** (lasting). Evidence: Steal a Brainrot, Pokemon TCG Pocket in the revenue charts.
   Appeals to: kids and collectors. Use it: unlock silly creatures as you play, shown in a collection book
   [p. 5].
6. **Absurd meme humour** (fad flavour). Evidence: brainrot's TikTok spread. Appeals to: kids and teens. Use
   it: invent your own ridiculous characters with funny names; don't copy existing memes [p. 5].
7. **Clip-worthy chaos** (lasting). Evidence: friendslop sells through funny clips; R.E.P.O. and PEAK's viral
   conversion. Appeals to: teens and streamers. Use it: physics mayhem, huge combos, spectacular fails worth
   recording [p. 5].
8. **Share-your-result daily challenges** (lasting). Evidence: Wordle's legacy; participation drives
   breakthroughs. Appeals to: adults. Use it: one seeded run per day with a shareable emoji grid [p. 5].
9. **Idle and incremental progress** (lasting). Evidence: prominent tags on itch.io's web charts. Appeals to:
   browser players. Use it: numbers that grow while you play, unlocking new mechanics [p. 5].
10. **Factory and automation** (lasting). Evidence: "factory" +325% on Steam. Appeals to: puzzle-minded
    adults. Use it: tiny conveyor-belt puzzles in a single screen [p. 5].
11. **Mystery and lore** (lasting). Evidence: Backrooms' 15 billion views; 71% watch fandom explainers.
    Appeals to: teens. Use it: hidden notes, secret rooms, a strange story players piece together [p. 5].
12. **Retro and analog nostalgia** (lasting). Evidence: Pinterest letter-writing and poetcore growth; "2026 is
    the new 2016." Appeals to: young adults. Use it: paper, stamps, old phone screens and pixel art as an art
    style [p. 5].
13. **Circus, opulence and alien-core aesthetics** (medium). Evidence: Pinterest search growth of 70-225%.
    Appeals to: style-led players. Use it: a fresh visual theme for an old mechanic [p. 5].
14. **Handmade feel** (lasting, and rising). Evidence: backlash against AI slop at Steam Next Fest. Appeals
    to: everyone. Use it: a consistent, intentional style; your own writing and voice; credits that show who
    made it [p. 5].
15. **Finishable games** (lasting). Evidence: live-service fatigue. Appeals to: adults. Use it: a clear ending
    in 15-60 minutes, with optional replay goals [p. 5].

### F7. The combination method: six steps, "about an hour of thinking" before Stage 1 of the roadmap [p. 5-6]

1. **Pick a classic** from your Remake Ladder, at the tier you're ready for. Write its core action in one
   sentence.
2. **Pick one twist** using the checklist in *Twisting the Classics*. It should change the shape of a session,
   not pile on rules.
3. **Pick one trend** from the list above. Prefer a *lasting* trend for the game's structure; use a fad only
   as flavour (names, jokes, art).
4. **Write the pitch:** "[Classic], but [twist], with [trend]." If it doesn't fit in one sentence, it's too
   big.
5. **Check it against *What Makes Games Great*:**
   - Is the core action still fun with plain shapes?
   - Does every action get instant, satisfying feedback?
   - Will players feel themselves getting better?
   - Is there at least one real decision?
   - Can a stranger start playing within 10 seconds?
   - Is there a natural place to stop?
6. **Run the idea filter.** If it passes, it goes into the project tracker and Stage 1 of the roadmap.

### F9. Idea filter [p. 9-10]

Run every concept through this before Stage 1. **If it fails two or more, rework it or pick another idea.**

**Is it fun?**
- [ ] The core action is fun in a plain-shapes prototype.
- [ ] The twist adds a decision, not just more rules.
- [ ] A stranger understands it from a five-second clip.

**Is it small enough?**
- [ ] The pitch fits in one sentence.
- [ ] It stays in its Remake Ladder tier, or one above at most.
- [ ] It's single-player, with no online servers.
- [ ] You can ship a first version in your deadline.

**Will it catch on?**
- [ ] It produces moments worth sharing: a big combo, a funny fail, a result grid.
- [ ] It's built on a *lasting* trend; any fad is only flavour you could swap out.
- [ ] It looks intentional and handmade, with a consistent style and your own writing.
- [ ] It's free or very cheap, and finishable.

### F10. Avoiding dead trends: four rules [p. 10]

1. **Fads are flavour, not foundations.** A game takes weeks; TikTok formats last days. Use a meme for a joke
   or a character name that you could swap for something else in an afternoon.
2. **Prefer what has lasted a year or more.** Cozy, roguelike runs, survivors-likes, puzzles and collecting
   have all been growing for years.
3. **Invent, don't borrow.** Make your own absurd creatures rather than using existing meme characters, which
   other people own and which date quickly.
4. **Check before you ship.** At Stage 7, ask Claude to search whether the trend you used is still alive, and
   adjust your store page wording if it has faded.

## What it means for Vampire: No Survivors

### (a) Process for an AI building alone

- The document expects a one-sentence pitch, the six-question check and the idea filter before any building
  [p. 5-6, 9]. Inference: the concept is already chosen, so use these as a self-audit recorded in the design
  document, not as a selection step.
- Prototype prompts share a pattern worth copying: "shapes only", "Include only this", "Tell me how to test
  it", and tunable content kept in a data table or list at the top of the file [p. 7-9].
- The design prompt asks for 5 pitches, each with tier, "the one decision it adds" and "the biggest risk",
  then one recommendation [p. 6]. Inference: a good header for each of the 3 loop variations.
- Trend data ages: re-check at ship time (Stage 7) [p. 10]; the guide itself wants a refresh every three to
  six months [p. 1].

### (b) Core loop, controls and feel

- The document's definition of the genre's use: "auto-attack, move to dodge, screen fills with enemies and
  effects"; its appeal is "power without hard controls" [p. 4].
- Roguelike layer: short runs with a pick-one-of-three upgrade after each wave [p. 4]; The Last Signal offers
  3 upgrades after each wave [p. 9].
- The loop must pass six checks: fun with plain shapes, instant satisfying feedback on every action, visible
  player improvement, at least one real decision, start within 10 seconds, a natural place to stop [p. 6].
- It must read from a five-second clip [p. 2, 9] and produce shareable moments: a big combo, a funny fail,
  chaos, a spectacle ending [p. 2, 5, 7, 10].
- A twist must change the shape of a session and add a decision, not pile on rules [p. 6, 9]. "Add theme and
  meaning (H)" is listed as a twist type [p. 9]. Inference: "you are the vampire hunting humans" should show
  up as a decision in play, not only as a reskin, or it fails the filter line "adds a decision".

### (c) Content, difficulty, builds and balance

- Structure of the hits: runs of minutes inside a bigger loop of unlocks and collections [p. 2].
- Finishable: a clear ending in 15-60 minutes with optional replay goals; no grinds, battle passes or fear of
  missing out [p. 2, 5].
- Optional layers the document rates as lasting: a collection book [p. 5]; lore scraps revealed per wave,
  hidden notes, a story to piece together [p. 5, 9]; one seeded run per day with a shareable result [p. 5, 8].
  Inference: lore fits a vampire theme; each layer is extra scope and should pass "small enough" first.
- Keep the upgrade list as data so more can be added later [p. 8].
- Nothing on difficulty curves, enemy counts, build depth or balance numbers.

### (d) Phone: layout, input, performance, audio

- Almost nothing. Mobile downloads are ruled by simple puzzles, mobile revenue by old giants, and hyper-casual
  hits churn fast [p. 1-2]. The one-minute session claim is tied to puzzles, not survivors-likes [p. 4].
- The survivors-like evidence is Steam only (Megabonk) [p. 1, 4]; the document gives no mobile or browser
  survivors-like data. The example prompts assume key presses [p. 7].

### (e) Testing and playtests

- Tests named: fun in a plain-shapes prototype; a stranger understands a five-second clip; a stranger starts
  within 10 seconds [p. 6, 9]. Each prototype prompt should end with how to test it [p. 7].
- The filter prompt demands honesty: which checks fail, biggest risk, smallest fix [p. 10]. Inference: the
  stranger and fun checks cannot be verified by an AI alone and belong in the owner's first test.

### (f) Market, positioning, name, release

- Rising or crowded? The document calls survivors-likes *lasting*, ranks them 2 of 15, says solo-made ones
  "still win" and that the genre has "been growing for years" [p. 1, 4, 10]. It says nothing about crowding,
  saturation, number of competitors or failure rates. Its whole case rests on one hit (Megabonk).
- Hits are remixes of proven formulas [p. 2], so building on a known genre is endorsed; copying is not:
  "Invent, don't borrow" [p. 10], "don't copy existing memes" [p. 5].
- Positioning levers: handmade feel (consistent intentional style, own writing and voice, credits that show
  who made it) against the AI slop backlash [p. 2, 5, 10]; finishable [p. 5]; free or very cheap, under $10
  [p. 2, 10]; single-player, no servers [p. 10].
- Release: re-check the trend and adjust store page wording if it has faded [p. 10].
- Name: no naming guidance in the document.

## Warnings and anti-patterns

- Building a game's structure on a fad; fads are only swappable flavour [p. 4, 6, 10].
- Using existing meme characters: others own them and they date quickly [p. 5, 10].
- A twist that piles on rules instead of adding a decision [p. 6, 9].
- A pitch that does not fit in one sentence is too big [p. 6].
- Going more than one Remake Ladder tier above your own; online servers; multiplayer [p. 10].
- Looking like low-effort AI output; players asked for an AI filter [p. 2].
- Live-service chores: grinds, battle passes, fear of missing out [p. 2].
- Hyper-casual hits enter and leave the charts quickly [p. 2].
- Trusting stale trend data; the list is a dated snapshot [p. 1, 2].
- The document separates hard data from opinion and marks some figures as company-stated or a live snapshot
  [p. 2, 10-11]; treat them with matching caution.

   > Build the prototype for "Night Garden": Pac-Man movement in one maze, shapes only. Eating a dot leaves a
   > flower behind. Four slow critters wander the maze; touching one scatters a few flowers instead of losing
   > a life. A 3-minute timer ends the night and shows the number of flowers. Include only this. Tell me how
   > to test it.

   > Build the prototype for "Big Top Hop": an endless upward Frogger. The player hops one tile per key press
   > across lanes of moving obstacles. Lanes are generated as you climb, and speed rises slowly. Score is rows
   > climbed. Shapes only, no art yet.

   > Build the prototype for "Ballstorm": Breakout with one paddle and one ball. When a wall of bricks is
   > cleared, pause and offer 3 upgrades as cards (extra ball, wider paddle, piercing ball). Then start a
   > harder wall. Keep the upgrade list in a data table at the top so we can add more later.

   > Build the prototype for "Snake of the Day": classic Snake where the food positions come from a seed based
   > on today's date, so everyone gets the same board. After the game, show the score and a "copy result"
   > button that copies a short emoji summary.

   > Build the prototype for "Line Works": Tetris rules, but each piece has an arrow showing a belt direction.
   > Packages appear at the left edge and move along connected belts. When a full line clears, every package
   > on it scores. Shapes only.

   > Build the prototype for "Space Critter Rescue": Asteroids controls and rocks. Small rocks, when broken,
   > release a critter that drifts away; flying into it catches it. Show a simple collection screen with 8
   > critter slots. Critters are colored circles with silly names for now.

   > Build the prototype for "The Last Signal": the player's cannon moves left and right and fires
   > automatically. Invaders descend in growing waves. After each wave, offer 3 upgrades and show one line of
   > a mysterious transmission. Shapes only; keep the transmission lines in a list at the top of the file.
