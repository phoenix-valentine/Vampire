# The Step 0 Guide: finding and defining a small game (pages 1 to 18)

Source: `c7129cb6-The_Step_0_Guide_finding_and_defining_a_small_game.pdf`, dated Nov 25, 2026 [p. 1]. All 18 pages read; none unreadable. Link targets are not visible in the page images, only the link text.

## What it is

A guide for "a solo beginner making small browser games" on what happens before any code: producing enough cheap ideas, then writing one page (the "Step 0 sheet") that says what the game is [p. 1]. It is written to the owner by an assistant ("my estimate", "your own Small Game Success Recipes checklist") [p. 6, 16] and is the paper form of an "Idea Interview" app [p. 1, 10]. Core claim: it is a numbers game. Make many cheap ideas, cut most early, define the survivor in a few plain sentences [p. 1]. The sheet's only job is to reach a playable prototype quickly [p. 14].

Short version [p. 1]: (1) Collect 10 or more one-line ideas in 15 minutes; (2) Cut to 3 with five yes-or-no questions; (3) Define each survivor on a Step 0 sheet; (4) Score each sheet with the 100-point checklist ("a score is a guide, not a verdict"); (5) Hand off the winner to step 1 as a starter prompt for a core-loop prototype. The whole run takes about an hour.

## Frameworks, lists and checklists

### Six seeds: pick one, then apply one or two remix moves [p. 1-2]

| Seed | Ask yourself | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A game you like | What one thing do I keep, and what one thing do I change? | Breakout, but broken bricks join your paddle |
| A feeling | What should the player feel in the first 30 seconds? | "Barely in control", "calm and tidy" |
| An action | What single move would feel good to repeat 500 times? | Fling, stack, slice, swing |
| A theme or word | What does this word make the player do? | "Gravity", "swarms", "10 seconds" |
| A limit | What if I only had one button, one screen or 60 seconds? | A shooter where you can't shoot |
| Surprise me | Which old or overlooked game could be remade small? | A pick from the Remake Ladder or Hidden Gems Pool |

### Six remix moves: turn one idea into five [p. 2]

1. Swap the theme: same rules, new setting.
2. Flip the goal: survive instead of win, lose on purpose, protect instead of attack.
3. Remove something: no jumping, no stopping, no second chance.
4. Combine two: one game's action with another game's goal.
5. Shrink it: one screen, one enemy type, one minute.
6. Change the failure: a timer instead of lives, a shrinking space instead of damage.

Getting many ideas quickly [p. 2-3]: go for count first (judge none until the list is full); work alone and in writing; set a mood target (music or art first); play it in your head (picture 30 seconds of play and the player's reaction); keep an idea journal.

The core sentence (one-liner shape): "[Action] to [goal], but [twist]." [p. 3]

### The nine parts of a Step 0 sheet, on one page [p. 3-4]

| Part | Question | Good answer | Weak answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Pitch | What is it, in one sentence? | "Drag to stack falling crates on a rocking boat, but every crate changes the tilt." | "A fun physics game with lots of levels." |
| 2. Action | What does the player do, again and again? | One verb and one object: drag crates | Three or more verbs |
| 3. Feeling | Why is it fun? | One feeling from the list below | "It's addictive" |
| 4. Base and twist | What is familiar, and what is new? | One known game type plus one change | All new, or nothing new |
| 5. Goal and failure | How do you win, lose and restart? | "Stack 10 to clear; the stack tipping ends the run; restart in one tap" | "There are many ways to play" |
| 6. Session | How long is one go? | 30 seconds to 3 minutes for a small browser game | "As long as you like" |
| 7. Look and sound | What does it look and sound like? | Three mood words, up to five colours, simple shapes | "Realistic graphics" |
| 8. Controls | How is it played on a phone and on a computer? | One thumb; the same action on mouse or keys | Needs a keyboard |
| 9. Smallest first version | What is in the first build, and what is left out? | The action as a toy, one goal, one way to fail, and a written "not yet" list | "Everything, but simple" |

Why one page (Stone Librande): long documents go unread; one page forces cutting what is not essential [p. 3].

### Six kinds of fun: pick one main and at most one more [p. 4]

MDA names eight; six fit small single-player games:
- Challenge: getting better at something hard ("one more try").
- Sensation: it looks, sounds and moves nicely (satisfying, juicy).
- Discovery: finding out what happens (curious, surprised).
- Expression: making something that is yours (proud, creative).
- Fantasy: pretending to be someone or somewhere else (powerful, cosy, sneaky).
- Pastime: relaxing and switching off (calm, tidy).

Narrative and Fellowship need more content or networking than a first small game should take on.

### Three pillars and the "not yet" list [p. 4]

- Pillars: three short rules about how the game should feel, such as "Always one tap from playing again". Every later feature helps a pillar, does nothing, or works against one; those working against a pillar are cut even when they are good ideas.
- "Not yet": what the first version leaves out (menus, levels, score saving, sound, extra enemies), written so cut ideas do not creep back. Order: build the action as a toy with no goal at all, then add the goal.

### Pass 1, the quick cut: 30 seconds per one-liner; fewer than four yeses, park it [p. 5]

1. Can I say what the player does in five words?
2. Can I picture the first 10 seconds of play?
3. Does it work with one thumb?
4. Could the first version be built from plain shapes in a weekend?
5. Would I want to play it myself?

Parked ideas stay in the library and often return as the twist for another idea.

### Pass 2, the 100-point checklist at step 0: 10 checks, 0-10 points each [p. 5-6]

Nothing is built yet, so each score is a prediction made from the sheet.

| # | Check | Judge it from | Can step 0 judge it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Verb in 5 seconds | Pitch, Action, Controls | Yes |
| 2 | Fun within 10 seconds | Smallest first version | Partly: plan for play on the first tap |
| 3 | Feedback on every input | Look and sound | No: decided in the build |
| 4 | Familiar base plus one twist | Base and twist | Yes |
| 5 | Session fits the device | Session | Yes |
| 6 | Cheap failure | Goal and failure | Yes |
| 7 | Choices that matter | Action, Goal and failure | Yes |
| 8 | Reason to return | Goal and failure | Yes |
| 9 | Readable look you can make | Look and sound | Yes |
| 10 | Built to spread | Pitch, Feeling | Partly |

Thresholds: 80 or more, build it; 65-79, fix the lowest checks on the sheet first; under 65, change the idea, not the code [p. 6].

A score is a guide, not a verdict [p. 6]: studios cut on play, not on paper (the paper score only decides which idea to prototype first); scores can be wrong in both directions; the score's real use is the weak spots (a 72 with two checks at 3 says what to fix); excitement counts (build the 75 you cannot stop thinking about, not the 85 you do not want to build).

### Workflow: seven steps [p. 6-7]

Seed 3 min; Collect 15 min (10 one-liners, judge nothing); Quick cut 5 min (keep the best 3); Define 10 min each; Score 5 min each (fix the lowest two checks on paper); Choose 2 min (park the others); Hand off 1 min (copy the starter prompt into a new chat). Times are the author's estimate, not measured [p. 6].

Rules that keep it fast [p. 7]:
- Use a timer. Short cycles did not produce worse games, longer ones did not produce better.
- Never fix a weak idea twice. Still under 65 after one round of fixes: park it.
- Decide alone, check with one person. Read the pitch to someone who does not play games; if they cannot repeat back what the player does, rewrite the pitch.
- One sheet in progress at a time.

### Hand-off to step 1 [p. 14-15]

A finished sheet hands over four things: the one action to build, the question the prototype must answer, the list of things to leave out, and a starter prompt.

| From the sheet | Becomes in step 1 |
|---|---|
| Action and Controls | The toy: the action working with touch and keys, no goal yet |
| Biggest risk | The one question the prototype exists to answer |
| Smallest first version | The build list |
| Not yet | The list you refuse to build this round |
| Pillars | The test for every change you are tempted to make |

"Step 1 ends with a decision, not a product: is the action fun for 60 seconds as plain shapes?" Yes: go on. No: take the next sheet [p. 15].

### The steps after step 0 [p. 15]

| Step | You make | Done when |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Define | A scored Step 0 sheet | The sheet fits one page and scores 65 or more |
| 1. Prototype | The action as a toy, in plain shapes | It is fun for 60 seconds, or you have parked it |
| 2. Make it a game | Goal, failure, restart, score | A stranger can play a full go without help |
| 3. Feel | Feedback on every input: motion, sound, timing | Every tap gets a visible and audible answer |
| 4. Content and difficulty | Levels or a generator, and a difficulty curve | Minute 5 is still interesting |
| 5. Look and sound | Final shapes, colours, sounds | It reads clearly in one screenshot |
| 6. Playtest | Notes from three or more people | The top three problems are fixed |

| 8. Release | An itch.io page | It is public, with a tagline and screenshots |

### Sources and the author's confidence [p. 16-17]

- Experimental Gameplay Project (Carnegie Mellon, 2005): four students, over 50 games, each by one person in under a week around a shared theme; none came from a scheduled brainstorm. Take: themes and limits help, ideas arrive alone, build the toy first. High.
- Supercell: small teams kill their own games, even ones that tested well; 4 published and 14 killed by 2016. Take: cutting is normal, the maker decides. High.
- Voodoo: about 2,000 prototypes tested a year; an extra round on a weak game counts as a lost chance. Take: don't fix a weak idea twice. Medium.
- Voodoo, ideation: look outside your genre, rework an older mechanic. Low (search summaries only).
- Double Fine, Amnesia Fortnight: since 2007 anyone can pitch; a few become two-week prototypes (4 of 23 in 2012). Take: many short pitches, few prototypes. High.
- Nintendo: Donkey Kong cut down to one move, jumping. Take: start from the action. Medium.
- MDA framework (Hunicke, LeBlanc, Zubek, 2004): players meet the feeling first, designers the rules first. Take: choose the feeling before the rules. High.
- Stone Librande; Unity: one-page designs; must, should, could and won't lists. High.
- Wireframe magazine: about three pillars, as short rules about feeling. High.
- Rami Ismail, Game a Week: ideas are vague until playable. High.
- Game jam advice: add your own limits, skip the obvious first idea, avoid 3D and multiplayer in small scopes. Medium.

The times, the quick-cut questions and the question bank are the author's own design, "not yet tested on your games" [p. 16].

### About "the budget"

The document has no money budget. Its budgets are time and scope: about an hour for step 0 [p. 1]; a first version buildable from plain shapes in a weekend [p. 5]; question 12 (One evening, A weekend, A week) "sets the size of the first build" [p. 12]; one page for the sheet; up to five colours; a session of 30 seconds to 3 minutes [p. 3].

## What it means for Vampire: No Survivors

### (a) Process for an AI building alone

- Write the one-page sheet before code and fill every line; a line that cannot be filled is the part of the idea that is not ready [p. 7].
- What to decide first: the Action line, with the world last [p. 14]; "What does your finger do most?" is the answer every other answer is built around [p. 10]; choose the feeling before the rules [p. 17].
- Score the sheet, then fix the two lowest checks on paper before building [p. 7]. The paper score only orders what to prototype; the real cut happens on play [p. 6]. Inference: the owner's test of the prototype is that cut.
- Three variations: the guide's rule is that each uses a different remix move, "so they differ in kind and not only in theme" [p. 13]. Inference: the three prototype variations should differ in a rule (goal, failure, something removed or combined), not in colours or tuning numbers.
- Stop at one page and build the toy; an hour on wording is the mistake the guide rates as mattering most [p. 14].
- "Check with one person" who does not play games [p. 7]. Inference: the lead has no such person; a fresh reviewer subagent given only the pitch is a proxy and must be reported as one.
- Inference: the game is already chosen by the owner, so idea collection and the quick cut matter less here than the sheet, the score and the hand-off.

### (b) Core loop, controls and feel

- One verb and one object [p. 3]. A pitch with "and" three times means several verbs: keep one, move the rest to Not yet [p. 14].
- Build the action as a toy with no goal, then add the goal [p. 4, 14]. Step 1 passes only if it is fun for 60 seconds in plain shapes [p. 15].
- The "Biggest risk" line becomes the one question the prototype answers [p. 15]. The starter prompt names "the thing to test", asks for tunables as named settings at the top, and ends "Stop there" [p. 10].
- Plan for play on the first tap (check 2); the verb must be clear in 5 seconds (check 1) [p. 5].
- One main kind of fun plus at most one more [p. 4]. Inference: for a vampire hunting humans the candidates are Fantasy ("powerful") with Sensation or Challenge; the lead must choose, not take all three.
- Feedback on every input cannot be judged at step 0 [p. 5]; the step 3 bar is that every tap gets a visible and audible answer [p. 15]. Inference: the owner asked for a "polished" plain-shapes loop, which in this guide's terms covers steps 1 to 3, more than the bare step 1 toy.
- Cheap failure: restart in one tap [p. 3, 9].
- Inference: none of question 4's six finger actions is exactly "hold and drag to steer"; the lead should write its own Action line.

### (c) Content, difficulty, builds and balance

- First version: one goal, one way to fail [p. 4]. "Shrink it": one screen, one enemy type, one minute [p. 2]. Extra enemies, levels, menus, score saving and sound are the guide's own "not yet" examples [p. 4].
- Session: 30 seconds to 3 minutes is the good answer for a small browser game, and it "must fit a phone" [p. 3, 12]. Inference: survivor-like runs are usually longer, so run length must be chosen deliberately and defended against check 5.
- Ways to get harder: things speed up, space runs out, more to watch at once, puzzles get trickier, it stays gentle [p. 13]. Step 4 is done when minute 5 is still interesting [p. 15].
- Reason to return is "the weakest check in most first ideas" [p. 12]; the worked example raised it with a daily shared seed [p. 9].
- "Choices that matter" is judged from Action plus Goal and failure [p. 6]. Inference: in a bullet heaven the build choices carry this check, so the sheet should say where the choice happens.
- The document says nothing specific about upgrades, builds or balance numbers.

### (d) Phone: layout, input, performance, audio

- One thumb; the same action on mouse or keys; "needs a keyboard" is weak [p. 3, 5].
- Prototype format: one HTML file with JavaScript and canvas, playable on phones and computers, plain rectangles and five flat colours [p. 10].
- Look: three mood words, up to five colours, simple shapes, drawable in code [p. 3, 12]; later it must read clearly in one screenshot [p. 15].
- Audio: the sheet names the 3 sounds that matter most [p. 8], but sound is left out of the first version [p. 4, 10].
- Performance appears only at step 7: smooth frame rate, runs well on an old phone [p. 15]. Inference: a bullet heaven has many objects on screen, so this cannot wait that long for this genre.
- Avoid 3D, online multiplayer and large amounts of content [p. 13, 17].

### (e) Testing and playtests

- Step 1: fun for 60 seconds as plain shapes, or park it. Step 2: a stranger can play a full go without help. Step 6: notes from three or more people, top three problems fixed [p. 15].
- Judge the idea as grey boxes; a setting cannot save weak play [p. 14].
- Inference: apart from the owner the lead has no human testers; these checks belong under "could not check" or "proxy only".

### (f) Market, positioning, name, release

- Base plus twist is one known game type plus one change [p. 3]. "All base, no twist" is a copy with different colours; "all twist, no base" means nobody can say what kind of game it is [p. 14].
- Theme is "the cheapest way to make a familiar game feel new" [p. 12] but cannot rescue weak play [p. 14]. Inference: "you are the vampire hunting humans" counts as the twist only if it changes what the player does (for example Flip the goal); as a re-skin it scores low on check 4.
- From a named game take how it plays, never its name, characters, art or levels [p. 13]. Inference: the working title sits close to a known game's title; the owner chose to keep it for now, so recheck before any public release.
- "Built to spread" is judged from Pitch and Feeling, only partly at step 0 [p. 6]; the example fix is a shareable picture of the result with the score [p. 9].
- Release here is an itch.io page, public, with a tagline and screenshots [p. 15].
- Supercell and Voodoo filter for large commercial hits; their kill rates are "a direction, not a target" [p. 18].

## Warnings and anti-patterns

The eight common mistakes [p. 14]:

| Mistake | How it shows | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Starting with the story or the world | The pitch describes a setting and no action | Write the Action line first; add the world last |
| Falling for the first idea | One idea, defended instead of compared | Write 10 before judging any |
| A pitch with "and" in it three times | Several verbs, several goals | Keep one verb; move the others to Not yet |
| All twist, no base | Nobody can say what kind of game it is | Name the familiar game type first |
| All base, no twist | It is a copy with different colours | Apply one remix move |
| Theme used as a rescue | A weak game with a clever setting | Judge the idea as grey boxes; a setting can't save weak play |
| No "not yet" list | The first version keeps growing | Write down what is left out before building |
| Polishing the sheet | An hour spent on wording, none on playing | Stop at one page and build the toy |

The last one matters most [p. 14]. Others:
- Treating the paper score as a verdict; it can be wrong in both directions [p. 6].
- A second round of fixes on a weak idea [p. 7].
- Keeping a good feature that works against a pillar [p. 4].
- Limits of the guide itself: survivorship (those who failed used the same methods); the studios cited have different goals; the quick-cut questions, times and question bank are untested; scores are predictions [p. 18].

Only one of 2a, 2b, 2c is asked [p. 13]. Question 5 "separates the five recipes from each other" [p. 11]; the recipes are in Small Game Success Recipes [p. 18].

Follow-ups [p. 13]:
- "How fast is it?" Fast reflexes; Steady rhythm; Slow and thoughtful. Asked when question 3 is anything except "Calm and tidy". Fills Feeling, Session.
- "What makes it harder as you go?" Things speed up; Space runs out; More to watch at once; Puzzles get trickier; It stays gentle. Asked when question 3 is "Tense", or a go ends on a mistake or a timer. Fills Goal and failure.

Quick mode asks question 1 (with its 2a, 2b or 2c), 3 and 4, then returns 10 one-liners [p. 13]. Rules for returned ideas [p. 13]: take how a named game plays, never its name, characters, art or levels; flag anything needing online multiplayer, 3D or a large amount of content and offer a smaller version; each of the three variations uses a different remix move.
