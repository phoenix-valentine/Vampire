# Less but Better: Simplifying Small Games (pages 1 to 15)

All 15 pages were read and were legible. Page 15 holds only the last three source entries.

## What it is

A short guide by the owner (tagged @Phoenix, dated Nov 4, 2026) with one job: take a game that has grown cluttered and make it simpler and better at the same time, and keep the next game from growing that way [p. 1]. It covers only simplicity, cutting and depth. General fun is left to "What Makes Games Great" and phone layout to "Mobile Games 101" [p. 1]. Each principle is laid out as What / Why / Examples / Apply, where Apply means "what it means for your one-file browser game" [p. 1]. Examples are mostly small or solo-made games: Threes!, Mini Metro, Downwell, Celeste, Tetris, Two Dots, Super Mario 3D Land, and one mention of Vampire Survivors [p. 2-9].

## Frameworks, lists and checklists

**When to use it** [p. 1]: first game, right now: sections 1 to 4, then the worksheet, then the Claude prompt with the game file. After cutting: sections 5 to 7. Before the next game: section 8.

**Three rules if you read nothing else** [p. 1]
1. Every feature must serve the core. If you can't say how it makes the main action better, it goes.
2. Fix, don't add. When the game feels weak, improve what's there before adding anything new.
3. Full comes from depth and polish, not from count. A few things that combine well and feel great beat many things that each feel thin.

**Spot it in your own game**: cluttered if several are true [p. 2-3]
- You can't explain the game in one sentence.
- A new player asks "what am I supposed to do?" or "what was that?"
- Two enemies or items do nearly the same thing.
- You struggle to see the player or the danger when lots is happening.
- Some features almost never come up in a normal run.
- Balancing one thing keeps breaking another.
- You keep adding effects because a moment "still doesn't feel right."

**Simple vs empty: the five qualities** [p. 3]. "Empty" does not come from having few things; it comes from missing one of these.

| Quality | Simple and full | Empty |
|---|---|---|
| Depth | Few rules, many interesting decisions | Few rules, one obvious move |
| Change | Situations keep shifting as you play | Minute 5 feels like minute 1 |
| Feel | Every action is satisfying to do | Actions work, but feel flat |
| Goal and ending | Clear target, clear finish, a reason to retry | No reason to keep going or to stop |
| Care | Consistent style, small surprises, details | Placeholder art, missing sounds, rough edges |

- 2.1: richness is the number of real choices, not the number of things. Apply: during a run, write down every choice; if most are obvious, the game needs more depth in the core, not more content [p. 3].
- 2.2: plan three stages for a run: calm start, building middle, intense end. Change the mix and the speed, not the rule set [p. 4].
- 2.3: everything that stays is finished: feels good, looks consistent, has sound. "Better five finished things than ten half-finished." Rough edges read as "lazy", no rough edges reads as "crafted" [p. 4].

**The core sentence (3.1)** [p. 4]: the core is the one action the player repeats most, plus what makes it interesting. Write it as: **"You [action] to [goal] while [pressure]."** Examples: Downwell: "You fall down a well, shooting below you, while enemies block the way." Mini Metro: "You draw subway lines to connect stations while the city keeps growing." Write it before touching any code. If it takes two sentences, you may have two games fighting each other; pick the one you like more. The sentence becomes the test for every feature.

**The core test (3.2)** [p. 4-5]: every feature must make the core action more **interesting** (new decisions), more **readable** (clearer), or more **satisfying** (better feel). If it does none of these, it goes. "It looks cool" is not one of the three. Reason given: features that don't touch the core take attention away from it (Sid Meier via Soren Johnson, 2009: the player should be the one having the fun).

**Two jobs (3.3)** [p. 5]: one feature doing two jobs is simpler than two features doing one each (Miyamoto's idea, quoted by Downwell's designer). Downwell's gunboots are weapon, fall brake and jump on one button. Questions to ask: could the attack also be the movement? Could the score pickup also heal? Could the hazard also be a tool? "Merging is often better than cutting."

**The cut pass, step by step** [p. 5-6]
1. Save a copy. Keep the current file as `game-v1-full.html`.
2. List everything. Play three runs and write down every enemy, item, power-up, effect, sound, screen, UI element and system.
3. Write the core sentence.
4. Sort each item into Keep, Merge, Cut or Polish using the worksheet.
5. Cut in this order, one change at a time, and play two or three runs after each:
   - Systems that don't touch the core (extra currencies, side modes, upgrade trees nobody uses)
   - Duplicates (merge or keep the better one)
   - Rare features (things that come up less than once a run)
   - Visual and sound noise (section 6)
   - Extra UI (section 6)
6. Re-tune with big changes. If something is off, double it or cut it in half instead of nudging by 5%.
7. Watch someone new play without explaining anything. Every "what's that?" points to something to clarify or cut.
8. Stop when the core sentence is true and nothing can be removed without being missed.

- 4.1: decide by removing and playing, not by thinking; the builder is attached, so guesses are biased. Remove it, play three runs. Didn't miss it: gone. Missed it: go to 4.2 [p. 6].
- 4.2: bring back the smallest version that gives what was missed (the feeling? the challenge? the reward?). Downwell's kill-combo was cut, then returned as an optional layer for skilled players, not something everyone must learn [p. 6].

**Depth from less (section 5)**: get more out of each thing instead of adding things [p. 6].
- 5.1 Combinations: ten things that never interact give ten situations; four things that all interact give many more, because every pair is a new problem. Tetris has seven pieces. "Vampire Survivors has one input (move), and the depth is in how weapons and items combine and evolve." Apply: make a small grid of kept enemies and hazards; for each pair ask "what new situation happens when these two meet?" and design for the interesting pairs [p. 7].
- 5.2 Variations: change one property of something the player already knows: faster, bigger, tougher, in groups, or in a new place. One base enemy plus two variations usually beats three unrelated enemies [p. 7].
- 5.3 Introduce, develop, twist, conclude (Nintendo's four steps; Hayashida, Super Mario 3D Land): teach it safely, use it in a harder setting, twist it in an unexpected way, then let the player show mastery. Apply: for each level or stage of the run, pick one thing and walk it through the four steps [p. 7].
- 5.4 The curve: control three dials: speed, number at once, and mix. Turn them up slowly through a run (calm, building, intense). Rising pressure replaces the urge to add something new every minute [p. 7-8].

**Visual and feedback clutter (section 6)**: said to be where "clunky" often really comes from: too many effects, colours and sounds competing on a small screen [p. 8].
- 6.1 Medium juice: a study of one action RPG at four effect levels (none, medium, high, extreme) found medium and high beat both none and extreme on every measure (Kao, 2020). Apply: turn every effect down to half, play, turn up only the ones you miss. Screen shake on more than two or three events: cut it from the rest [p. 8].
- 6.2 Tracking limit: working memory holds about four chunks (Cowan, 2001); past that players stop reading and start guessing. Apply: at most about four kinds of moving thing on screen in normal play; UI 3 or 4 items during play [p. 8].
- 6.3 Brightness ladder: importance must match brightness and contrast. Top: player and danger (brightest, most contrast). Middle: rewards and pickups. Bottom: background and decoration (dark, low contrast, slow). Pick 3 to 5 colours and give each a meaning. Downwell: black, white and one red for danger and rewards [p. 8-9].
- 6.4 Sound: one sound per kind of event. Don't play the same sound more than two or three times at once. Most important events loudest, most frequent ones quietest. Two Dots plays one rising note per connected dot, so the sound tells the size of the move [p. 9].

**Polish order (section 7)**: each step touches more of the player's time than the next [p. 9].

| Order | Polish | Why first |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The core action | The player does it hundreds of times a run |
| 2 | The first 10 seconds | Decides whether they stay at all |
| 3 | Losing and restarting | Happens every run; must be quick and fair |
| 4 | Readability | The player must always see themselves and the danger |
| 5 | One or two signature moments | The big win or best score: what people remember |
| 6 | Consistency | One art style, one font, one sound style |

- 7.1: spend a whole session on only the core action: its timing, its sound, its little squash, its hit effect [p. 10].
- 7.2 Invisible help: coyote time, input buffering, hazard hitboxes a little smaller than they look, "where they fit". Nobody notices them, everyone notices when they are missing [p. 10].
- 7.3: polish is removing rough edges, not adding effects. Hunt for bugs, jitter, missing sounds, odd timings and overlapping text before adding any new effect [p. 10].

**Staying simple (section 8)**
- 8.1 Starting budget for a small game: one core action, 3 enemy or obstacle types (plus variations), 1 or 2 pickup types, 3 to 5 colours, 3 or 4 UI items during play. Write it in the GOOD file [p. 11].
- 8.2: first build is coloured boxes, the core action and a score. Only start adding once someone plays it three times in a row without being asked. If the core isn't fun, no amount of content fixes it; if it is, far less content is needed than expected [p. 11].
- 8.3: new ideas go into an IDEAS list, not the game. One in, one out. Waiting a day shows which still matter. Run the core test before adding. Leftovers become updates or the next game [p. 11].
- 8.4: Claude checks every new feature against core and budget before writing code [p. 11-12].

## What it means for Vampire: No Survivors

### (a) Process for an AI building alone
- Write the core sentence before any code and use it as the gate for every feature [p. 4]. Inference: if three prototype variations are built, each needs its own single sentence; a variation that needs two sentences is two games [p. 4].
- Fix the budget in the design document before building [p. 11]. Inference: the document's "GOOD file" maps to `docs/DESIGN.md`, and its IDEAS section to a parking-lot list there.
- The document warns that with Claude features are cheap to add and Claude "will happily add whatever you ask", which makes clutter fast [p. 2, 11]. The self-check it prescribes: how does the feature serve the core sentence, what does it cost in clarity, what could be removed instead [p. 12]. Inference: with no owner approval step, write those three answers down before coding instead of waiting for an OK.
- When something feels weak, improve the existing part; ask what problem the proposed addition was meant to fix, since the fix often belongs in the core [p. 2].
- Cut one change at a time with a backup file, and re-tune by doubling or halving [p. 5-6].
- "Cut by playing, not by arguing" assumes a human playing [p. 6]. Inference: bot runs are only a proxy for this.

### (b) Core loop, controls and feel
- The first build the document asks for is exactly coloured boxes, the core action and a score [p. 11].
- Polish in the fixed order: core action, first 10 seconds, losing and restarting (quick and fair), readability, one or two signature moments, consistency [p. 9].
- Give the core action a whole session: timing, sound, squash, hit effect [p. 10].
- Look for two-job mechanics: attack as movement, score pickup as heal, hazard as tool [p. 5]. Inference: blood that is both experience and health, or a lunge that is both dodge and bite, are the vampire-shaped versions.
- Invisible help "where they fit" [p. 10]. Inference: in a one-thumb survivor-like the fitting one is hazard hitboxes smaller than they look; coyote time and input buffering are jump-game tools.
- Medium juice: start effects at half and shake on at most two or three events [p. 8].
- The core action must feel great "on its own, even with nothing else happening" [p. 13].

### (c) Content, difficulty, builds and balance
- The document's one remark on the genre: Vampire Survivors has one input, and its depth is in how weapons and items combine and evolve [p. 7]. Depth should come from pairs that interact, checked with a pair grid [p. 7].
- Start with 3 enemy types plus variations (faster, bigger, tougher, in groups, in a new place) and 1 or 2 pickup types [p. 7, 11].
- Shape each run as calm, building, intense using speed, number at once and mix, not new rules [p. 4, 8]. Give each stage one idea walked through introduce, develop, twist, conclude [p. 7].
- Count decisions: if most upgrade or movement choices are obvious, add depth to the core, not content [p. 3].
- Systems that don't touch the core are cut first: extra currencies, side modes, upgrade trees nobody uses. The worksheet's own example cuts a between-runs coin shop and parks it in IDEAS [p. 5, 12]. Inference: any meta-progression must pass the core test or wait.
- Anything that appears less than once a run is a cut candidate [p. 5].
- Clear target, clear finish, a reason to retry [p. 3].

### (d) Phone: layout, input, audio
- Clutter is worst on a small screen [p. 8]. Keep UI to 3 or 4 items during play and about four kinds of moving thing [p. 8].
- Brightness ladder: player and danger on top, pickups in the middle, background dark, low contrast and slow; 3 to 5 colours with fixed meanings [p. 9].
- Audio: one sound per event kind, the same sound at most two or three times at once, important loud, frequent quiet [p. 9]. Inference: in a horde game, hits, kills and pickups are the frequent events, so they are the quiet, voice-capped ones; level-up, taking damage and death are the loud ones.
- Constraint as a source of design: Downwell's gunboots came from a portrait phone screen with only three inputs [p. 5, 11].
- Layout detail is deferred to Mobile Games 101 [p. 1, 8]. Nothing on performance.

### (e) Testing and playtests
- Watch a new player with no explanation; each "what's that?" is something to clarify or cut [p. 6].
- The gate for adding content is a person replaying three times in a row unasked [p. 11, 13]. Inference: this can only be checked in the owner's test of the prototype; it is "could not check" for the AI.
- Use the "Spot it" list and the Full-not-empty checklist as review rubrics [p. 2-3, 13].
- Log every choice made in a run to measure depth [p. 3].

Part (f) is left out: the document says nothing on market, name or release, beyond its prompt naming itch.io as the target [p. 14].

## Warnings and anti-patterns

- Adding feels like progress but makes the game harder to read, balance and finish [p. 2].
- The "Covert Action rule": parts that are each fun can "fight with each other" when combined [p. 2].
- Adding on top of a weak part hides the problem. "If the game isn't compelling enough to hold someone's attention, then the game needs to be better", not bigger [p. 2].
- Adding effects because a moment "still doesn't feel right" [p. 3].
- Minute 5 feeling like minute 1 [p. 3].
- Keeping a feature because "it looks cool" [p. 5].
- Nudging balance by 5% [p. 6].
- Deciding cuts by argument, and restoring a cut feature whole when only one part mattered [p. 6].
- Forcing every player to learn a layer that could be optional for skilled players [p. 6].
- Maximum juice: too much feedback hides the game [p. 8].
- Everything bright, so nothing stands out and the player cannot find themselves or the danger [p. 8].
- Many overlapping sounds turning into noise [p. 9].
- "Polish" used as a reason to add effects; that is how clutter comes back [p. 10].
- Trying to fix an unfun core with content [p. 11].
- Putting ideas straight into the game while they feel urgent [p. 11].
- Placeholder art, missing sounds, jitter and overlapping text read as lazy [p. 3, 13].

## Prompts and templates in the document

**1. Session starter line** (makes Claude an editor) [p. 12], in full:
"Before adding any feature, tell me how it serves the core sentence, what it costs in clarity, and what I could remove instead. Then wait for my OK."

**2. Core sentence template** [p. 4, 12]: "You ________ to ________ while ________."

**3. Cut audit worksheet** [p. 12-13]. Header: Game; Core sentence; Budget: core actions __ · enemy or obstacle types __ · pickup types __ · colours __ · UI items during play __. How to decide:
- Keep: clearly makes the core more interesting, readable or satisfying.
- Merge: overlaps with something else; combine them into one.
- Cut: none of the three, or rarely comes up. Remove it and play three runs.
- Polish: stays, but feels rough.

Columns: Feature | Kind | Serves the core how? (interesting / readable / satisfying / none) | How often per run | Decision | Notes. Example rows: Spiky enemy, Enemy, "Interesting: forces you to jump", Often, Keep, "Make a fast variation instead of the bat". Bat enemy, Enemy, "Same job as spiky", Often, Merge, becomes "fast spiky". Coin shop, System, None, Between runs, Cut, "Park in IDEAS". Death effect, Effect, "Satisfying, but hides the restart", Every run, Polish, "Half the particles, shorter". Footer: items kept __ · merged __ · cut __ · to polish __. Does the core sentence still describe the game? Y / N.

**4. Full-not-empty checklist** [p. 13]. Run after the cut pass; if a box stays empty, fix it by improving what's there, not by adding something back.

Simple
- I can explain the game in one sentence, and that sentence is true
- Every feature makes the core more interesting, readable or satisfying
- No two things do the same job
- About four kinds of moving thing at most during normal play; 3 or 4 UI items
- I can always see myself and the danger, even at the busiest moment
- Effects are medium, not maximum; screen shake only on the biggest events

Full
- Most moves are real decisions, not obvious ones
- Kept pieces combine into new situations
- A run feels different at the end than at the start (calm, building, intense)
- Clear goal, clear ending, a reason to try again
- The core action feels great on its own, even with nothing else happening
- One or two big moments players will remember
- No rough edges: no placeholder art, missing sounds, jitter or overlapping text
- A new player played it three times in a row without being asked

**5. Prompt: audit my game for cuts** [p. 13-14], used with the game's HTML file attached, in full:

```
I want to simplify this game so it feels clean and focused, especially on a
phone, without it feeling empty. It is a single HTML file (JavaScript and
canvas) for itch.io.

Core sentence: You ____ to ____ while ____.

Don't change any code yet. First:
1. List every feature in the game: enemies, items, systems, effects, sounds,
screens, UI.
2. For each, say whether it makes the core more interesting, more readable or
more satisfying, or none. Flag duplicates and things that rarely come up.
3. Suggest Keep, Merge, Cut or Polish for each, with one line of reason.
Point out any two features that could merge into one.
4. Name the 3 cuts that would help most, in order, and what to check after
each.
5. Suggest 2 ways to add depth with what stays (combinations, variations,
difficulty curve), not with new features.
6. List visual and sound clutter: effects to shorten or remove, colours to
merge, UI to hide.

Present this as a table plus short notes. Wait for my OK, then make one
change at a time so I can play-test between them. Keep a backup of the
original.
```

## Things the lead should decide or check

1. The core sentence for the game, and one per prototype variation [p. 4].
2. The written budget. The document's default is 1 core action, 3 enemy types plus variations, 1 or 2 pickups, 3 to 5 colours, 3 or 4 UI items [p. 11]. Inference: a bullet heaven's weapon and upgrade list is not covered by this budget; set an explicit number for it.
3. "About four kinds of moving thing" [p. 8]. Inference: player, humans, own attacks, pickups already make four; hostile projectiles, damage numbers and particles would exceed it, so decide which get merged or pushed down the brightness ladder.
4. Which two or three events get screen shake [p. 8], and which one or two moments are the signature ones [p. 9].
5. The colour meanings and the ladder: is the player always findable at peak density [p. 9, 13].
6. The sound voice cap and loudness order [p. 9].
7. Templates 1 and 5 end in "wait for my OK" [p. 12, 14]. Inference: this conflicts with working without approval; decide how the check is logged instead.
8. How to stand in for "watch someone new play" and "three times in a row without being asked" [p. 6, 11], and report them as not checked until the owner plays.
9. Whether any between-runs system passes the core test or goes to IDEAS [p. 5, 12].
10. Tension to resolve: plain shapes are the prescribed first build [p. 11], yet "placeholder art, missing sounds, rough edges" is what reads as empty [p. 3]. Inference: the prototype should be plain but precise and consistent, with sound, like the Mini Metro example [p. 4, 10].
11. The juice study is cited through a secondary summary [p. 15]; treat "medium and high beat none and extreme" as the document's claim, not as checked.
