## What it is

The second half of a guide for a solo beginner who builds games with Claude and wants income from them. This half is mostly "Sell" and "Grow" (store cuts, marketing, publishing per platform, legal and AI disclosure, after launch, a 12-month playbook), then "Reference" (mistakes table, tool stack, prompt library, templates, glossary, sources, caveats). The parts that bear on building are Phase A of the playbook [p. 38], the mistakes table [p. 41], the prompt library [p. 43-44] and the templates [p. 45]. The guide says it is general information, not legal, tax or financial advice [p. 33, 48], and that prices and figures are as of Oct-Nov 2026 and change often [p. 42, 48].

## Frameworks, lists and checklists

### Phase A: first game in 2 weeks ($0-20) [p. 38]

1. Day 1: get Claude Pro (or start free). Paste the session starter and scope-cutter prompts. Pick a one-sentence core loop with 3 controls at most.
2. Days 2-4: prototype with shapes only. Play it 10 times. If it isn't fun after 3 tweaks, change the core action.
3. Days 5-6: complete the loop: title, play, game over, restart, score, difficulty ramp.
4. Days 7-9: content (3 kinds of each at most). Then the fun-pass prompt.
5. Days 10-11: juice. A 4-colour palette, code-drawn art, coded sounds, a mute key, touch controls.
6. Days 12-13: feature freeze. 3 strangers play without help; fix the top 3 problems.
7. Day 14: publish on itch.io, disclose AI use, share the link, write a 3-line retro. Post a clip.

### 12-month roadmap [p. 38]

A. First game (weeks 1-2): a finished browser game on itch.io. B. Games 2-3 + audience start (weeks 3-8): two more games, one on a web portal; first followers. C. Commercial mobile game (months 3-6): validated idea, soft-launched with retention data. D. Global mobile launch + iterate (months 6-9). E. Steam version (months 6-12). F. Switch (month 12+).

Phase B: each game adds one new skill (a sprite set, then a music track, then analytics events, then ads via a portal SDK); submit the best to CrazyGames or Poki; enter one game jam [p. 38].

### Phase C steps [p. 39]

1. Pick and validate (2-3 weeks): 10 ideas -> top 3 -> clip tests -> choose one with a hybrid-casual shape (simple core + meta progression).
2. Design the economy now (rewarded ads, what you sell, the meta loop).
3. Build the vertical slice (4-6 weeks): core loop + first 10 minutes polished + meta loop working. Add analytics on day one.
4. Art direction pass: lock palette and style; consider a human-made icon.
5. Soft launch (3-4 weeks): read day-1/day-7 retention against thresholds set in advance.
6. Decide: hit thresholds -> launch. Close -> fix onboarding and test again. Far -> keep the lessons and move on.

### Weekly rhythm [p. 39-40]

Building and polishing ~50%; marketing ~25-30%; playtesting and analytics ~10-15%; learning ~10%. The guide calls this a recommendation.

### What to sell (tail of Part 7) [p. 25]

Cosmetics are the least resented purchases. Convenience is fine if free players can still progress. Season or battle pass only once you can ship regular content. Avoid pay-to-win and loot boxes.

### Marketing timeline [p. 27]

Idea stage: post prototype clips, test hooks, one consistent account name. Early production: "Coming Soon" page or pre-registration, Discord, 3-5 short clips a week. Mid production: devlogs, playtests, emails, contact creators. 2-3 months before launch: demo or soft launch, a festival. Launch: trailer, press kit, creator keys, launch post, discount. After: updates, sales, events, cross-promotion.

Short-form video [p. 27]: the same vertical clip works on TikTok, YouTube Shorts and Reels. Hook in the first second: the funniest crash, the most satisfying moment, or an on-screen question. Formats that work: before/after progress, "I made X in Y days", bugs and fails, challenges, "solo dev" story. Games go viral when the game itself produces clips worth sharing (gem: "clip-first development").

Trailers [p. 29]: gameplay in the first 3 seconds, no slow logos; 30-60 seconds; hook, variety, call to action; real gameplay only, never AI-generated video.

### Store page checklist (15.4) [p. 45]

- Capsule/icon readable at thumbnail size and in the game's real style
- First video shows gameplay in the first second
- Screenshots 1-2 show the hook; captions short
- Short description: genre + hook + why it's fun
- Tags/keywords match the audience; searched competitor terms
- AI statement specific and honest; links to Discord, socials and mailing list

Mobile ASO adds [p. 28]: title and subtitle with the words players search for (genre + hook); test icon variants; ask for ratings only after a positive moment (a win), never on first launch.

### Web portals and itch.io [p. 30-31]

itch.io: upload an HTML project as a zip; free; same-day; no discovery, you bring the players; AI disclosure mandatory for asset packs, optional for games, "disclose anyway". Poki: curated, every game human-reviewed, free to submit, SDK for ads, review takes weeks. CrazyGames: quality review, SDK for ads. Requirements in common: fast load, works on mobile browsers, fun in the first seconds, no external ads.

### AI disclosure: Do and Don't (11.2) [p. 36]

Do: disclose specifically (what was AI, what was you); show your hand (devlogs, personal note, credits naming tools and choices); put human effort where players look (art direction, game feel, the first 60 seconds, icon and capsule, trailer); consider a human artist for icon or key art; make AI part of the concept when it fits; polish and respond (fix reported bugs fast).

Don't: hide AI use; market "100% AI-made"; ship AI voices without a human pass; use generated video in trailers; over-disclose what Valve doesn't ask for (AI coding help).

Store rules [p. 33]: Steam requires disclosure of pre-generated AI content that ships or appears in marketing (art, audio, text) and of live-generated content; AI coding tools are exempt; failing to disclose can get a game pulled. Apple has no public AI-content disclosure rule. EU AI Act Article 50: for creative works the duty is limited to disclosing that AI-generated content exists; a short honest statement on the store page and in credits covers the spirit.

Ownership [p. 33-34]: works generated entirely by AI are not copyrightable in the US; human contributions (code edits, level design, writing, selection, arrangement, modification) are protected. Never prompt for a named game's characters or art.

### Asset and licence ledger columns (10.4) [p. 34]

File name; Type (sprite / music / SFX / model / text); Tool and model; Plan (free / paid, with date); Licence terms (commercial? credit required?); Prompt or source; Human edits made; Disclosed on store? (Y/N). Keep it from day one.

### Names and trademarks (10.5) [p. 34]

Search the stores, Google and trademark databases (USPTO, EUIPO) before naming. Avoid names close to famous games; that is also how "copycat" listings get rejected or buried. Secure matching social handles and a domain early.

### Launch week (12.1) [p. 37]

Watch crash reports and reviews daily, fix within days; reply politely to negative reviews with what you fixed; post clips daily; check analytics (tutorial drop-off, first-session length, day-1 retention); write down what you would do differently.

First 90 days, web portals [p. 37]: watch play time and ad revenue per player; small balance tweaks can change portal ranking.

### Launch checklist (13.9) [p. 40]

- AI disclosure complete and honest
- Asset and licence ledger up to date; no free-tier assets in a paid build
- Store art matches the in-game style; trailer made from real gameplay
- Privacy policy, data forms and age ratings complete (mobile)
- Analytics events verified live; crash reporting on
- Tested on low-end and high-end devices; controller support if listed
- Launch post per community; creator emails sent
- Launch discount or launch event ready
- A first-week patch plan and time blocked to respond to players

### Tool stack, free column (14.2) [p. 41-42]

Engine: HTML5 + Phaser/three.js. Mobile wrapper: Capacitor. 2D art: code-drawn art, Kenney CC0. SFX: jsfxr, ChipTone, coded sounds. Music: coded music, CC0 loops. Analytics: GameAnalytics or Firebase. Publishing: itch.io, web portals. "Lean" stack is about $20/mo: Claude Pro + code art + coded sound + CC0 music [p. 42].

### Glossary items worth keeping (16.1) [p. 46]

Core / session / meta loop: the repeated action (seconds) / one run (minutes) / long-term progress (days). Juice: small effects (shake, particles, sound) that make actions feel satisfying. Vertical slice: a small, fully polished piece that represents final quality. Hybrid-casual: simple casual core plus deeper progression. CLAUDE.md: a file of project rules Claude Code reads automatically. Capacitor: wraps a web game as an iOS or Android app.

## What it means for Vampire: No Survivors

### (a) Process for an AI building alone

- One feature or fix at a time; keep everything else working exactly as it is [p. 43].
- Before changing code, state in 2-3 lines what changes and why [p. 43]. Inference: with no reader, put this in the commit message or step log.
- Keep tweakable numbers in one settings section at the top with plain comments; juice strengths go there too [p. 43, 44].
- After each change, produce a short test list [p. 43] and run it (inference: the lead runs it, since no human will).
- Do not stack fixes on failed fixes; roll back after 2 failures [p. 41].
- Bug discipline: find and explain the cause first, then fix only that [p. 44].
- Scope: core loop in one sentence, up to 5 must-haves, a Later list, top 3 risks, and kill criteria set before testing [p. 43, 45].
- No giant one-shot prompt; it gives generic results that cannot be steered [p. 41]. Max effort is not the default [p. 41].
- Freeze features before the outside test [p. 38].
- Passes are ordered: shapes prototype -> complete loop -> content -> fun pass -> juice -> freeze and test [p. 38].

### (b) Core loop, controls and feel

- Write the loop as "The player does X to get Y while avoiding Z" [p. 43, 45].
- Prototype with shapes only and judge fun there; the test is 10 plays and at most 3 tweaks before changing the core action [p. 38]. This matches the owner's first stop. Inference: three variations should differ in the core action, not in tuning.
- A complete loop means title, play, game over, restart, score, difficulty ramp [p. 38].
- Juice is added without changing gameplay: 60 ms hit-pause, small screen shake on impact, particles on collect, squash-and-stretch, rising-pitch sound for combos, light haptics [p. 44]. The guide places juice after content [p. 38]; the owner asked for a polished loop first, so the lead must choose (see last section).
- Spend the most care on the first 60 seconds and on game feel [p. 36].

### (c) Content, difficulty, builds and balance

- Early content cap: 3 kinds of each at most [p. 38].
- Shape to aim at: simple core + meta progression; the GDD template asks for a session loop and a meta loop ("why come back tomorrow") [p. 39, 45].
- Small balance tweaks move play time, which moves portal ranking [p. 37].
- The document gives no formulas for difficulty curves, builds or balance.

### (d) Phone: layout, input, performance, audio

- Mobile-ready list: touch controls with large hit areas in the bottom third, safe-area margins, auto-pause when the app loses focus, save progress, a mute button that remembers its setting, steady 60 fps, then a list of what to test on a real phone [p. 44].
- Audio at this budget: coded sounds, coded music or CC0 loops, jsfxr, ChipTone [p. 38, 42].
- Art at this budget: code-drawn, 4-colour palette [p. 38, 42].
- Portal bar: fast load, works on mobile browsers, fun in the first seconds [p. 31].
- Test on low-end and high-end devices [p. 40].
- Inference: the guide's "save after every level" maps to saving after each run and each meta change; haptics may be unavailable in iPhone Safari, so check before relying on them.

### (e) Testing and playtests

- Self-test: 10 plays of the prototype [p. 38].
- Outside test: 3 strangers without help, fix the top 3 problems [p. 38]. Inference: the project has only the owner as a human tester; say so plainly.
- Record with the playtest notes form: time to first action, finished first level, total play time, where they hesitated, failed, smiled, and three questions [p. 45].
- If analytics are added later, the named events and metrics are tutorial drop-off, first-session length, day-1 retention [p. 37, 44].

### (f) Market, positioning, name, release

- Name: the guide warns against names close to famous games [p. 34] and lists "cloning a specific game" as a top mistake (Claude Code refuses, IP risk, "slop" label, Apple 4.3) [p. 41]. Inference: "Vampire: No Survivors" sits close to a famous title; the owner chose to keep it for now, so log the risk and revisit before any release.
- Cheapest release path for a single HTML file: itch.io zip, then one non-exclusive portal; Poki pays 100% on traffic you bring [p. 25, 31].
- The game should produce shareable 10-second clips with a hook in the first second [p. 27, 43].
- Disclose AI art, audio and text specifically; AI coding help need not be volunteered on Steam [p. 33, 36].
- Paid installs rarely pay back for a beginner [p. 26]; most first games earn under $100 [p. 41].
- Repo rule says no publishing unless the owner asks; this part is for later.

## Warnings and anti-patterns

"What doesn't work" (14.1), all rows [p. 41]:

| Mistake | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| One giant prompt for a "AAA" game, then trying to sell it | Burns a whole session per build; generic results you can't steer |
| Cloning a specific game | Claude Code refuses; IP infringement; players call it slop; Apple 4.3 rejections |
| Building in secret, marketing at the end | No followers means no launch |
| Hiding AI use, or bragging "100% AI" | Both backfire |
| Free-tier assets in commercial builds | No commercial rights; upgrading later doesn't fix it |
| Max effort as your default | ~10-20x the cost of medium for marginal gains |
| Stacking fixes on failed fixes | Code rot; roll back after 2 failures |
| Starting in Unreal, or switching engines mid-project | Slowest with AI; switching costs more than any gain |
| Mixed art styles, glossy generic AI art, AI text in images | The clearest "slop" signals |
| Bolting ads onto a finished mobile game | Intrusive placements; poor revenue; bad reviews |
| Scaling paid installs before retention is proven | CPI of $5-20 vs lifetime value of cents |
| Relying on Next Fest to create an audience | It multiplies the audience you bring |
| Generic AI-written store copy and screenshots | Triggers Apple 4.3 and turns players away |
| Planning Switch first | Prove the game elsewhere first |
| Spending more on tokens than a realistic revenue estimate | Most first games earn under $100 |

Other warnings:

- Apple guideline 4.3 "spam" is the most common rejection for AI-era apps; fixes are original screenshots, specific descriptions, real native features (haptics, Game Center, widgets) and a clear note in "Notes for Review" [p. 31].
- "Slop" is about carelessness, not the tool; players forgive AI-assisted code far more than visible AI art [p. 36].
- Don't underprice to signal "cheap" [p. 25].
- Avoid targeting children unless planned for; child-directed rules are stricter [p. 35].
- Caveats: much tool coverage comes from vendors; "which AI makes the best games" rests mainly on one expert's reports; revenue figures are mostly self-reported or estimated; model names, effort defaults and prices are likely outdated soon [p. 48].

## Prompts and templates in the document

All in 15.1 [p. 43-44] unless noted.

**Idea generator** (plan): "I want to make a [mobile / Steam] game I can finish in [time]. My interests: [list]. Give me 10 ideas. For each: one-sentence core loop ("player does X to get Y while avoiding Z"), the one twist, a reference game players already buy, why it would make good 10-second clips, and the biggest risk. Then rank the top 3 for a beginner solo developer and say why."

**Scope cutter** (plan): "Here's my idea: [describe it]. Act as a strict producer. Cut it to the smallest version that's still fun: core loop in one sentence, up to 5 must-have features, and a Later list. Then list the 3 biggest risks for a solo beginner."

**Session starter (beginner mentor mode)** (build): "You're my game dev mentor. I'm a beginner who can't read code well yet. We're building [game] in [engine / one HTML file with JavaScript and canvas]. RULES: 1. Before changing code, tell me in 2-3 lines what you'll change and why. 2. One feature or fix at a time. Keep everything else working exactly as it is. 3. Keep tweakable numbers in a settings section at the top, with plain-English comments. 4. After each change, give me a short test list. 5. If my request is too big for my scope or deadline, say so and suggest a smaller version."

**One-shot opener (Willison)**: an example of a short first prompt for a three.js game ("Tree appreciator") that ends "Think hard about how to make sure it is both fun and beautiful."

**Demo to game**: "Now turn this into an actual game - quadruple the size of the playable area, introduce fun game mechanics, add a plot that isn't too cliche, some twists, a magic system, make it really good"

**Fun pass**: "This game isn't fun enough yet. Make it a LOT more fun."

**Juice pass**: "Add juice without changing gameplay: a 60ms hit-pause and small screen shake on impact, particles on collect, squash-and-stretch on jump and land, a rising-pitch sound for combos, and light haptic feedback on mobile. Put every strength value in the settings block so I can tune them."

**Mobile-ready pass**: "Make this game mobile-ready without changing the gameplay: touch controls with large hit areas in the bottom third, safe-area margins, auto-pause when the app loses focus, save progress after every level, a mute button that remembers its setting, and a steady 60 fps on a mid-range Android phone. Then list what I should test on a real phone."

**Asset handoff**: swaps the player rectangle for a sprite sheet. "Attached: hero_run_32x32_8f.png, an 8-frame run cycle, 32x32 per frame, in one row. Replace the player rectangle with this animation at 10 frames per second, scaled 3x with crisp pixels (no smoothing). Keep all gameplay the same, and tell me how to test it."

**Bug report**: "BUG. What I did: [steps]. What I expected: [x]. What happened: [y]. Error text: [paste console / output]. Device: [phone / browser / OS]. Recent change: [what changed]. Find the cause first and explain it, then fix only this and keep everything else the same."

**Analytics setup**: "Add [GameAnalytics / Firebase] to this game. Track exactly these events: tutorial_step (with step number), tutorial_complete, level_start, level_complete, level_fail (with level number), ad_offer_shown, ad_watched, store_open, purchase. Explain how I can verify each one in the live dashboard."

**Store page draft** (sell; rewrite in your own voice): "Write 3 options for my store short description (max 2 lines each) for [game]: genre, the one hook, and why it's fun. Avoid buzzwords and generic phrases. Then suggest 5 screenshot captions of 3-5 words each."

**One-page game design document (15.2)** [p. 45], fields in order: Title; Platform(s); Target session (min); 10-second pitch; Core loop ("The player does ___ to get ___ while avoiding ___."); The one twist; Reference games players already buy; Session loop; Meta loop (why come back tomorrow); Must-haves (max 5); Later list; Art style + palette; Audio approach; Monetization (rewarded ads at ___; purchases ___; or premium price); Controls (touch / controller / keyboard); Top 3 risks; Kill criteria (set before testing).

**Playtest notes form (15.3)** [p. 45]: Tester (stranger / friend); Device; Build; Time to first action (s); Finished first level? Y/N; Total play time (min); Where they hesitated; Where they failed; Where they smiled; Q1 What was the game about? Q2 What was frustrating? Q3 Would you play again tomorrow?
