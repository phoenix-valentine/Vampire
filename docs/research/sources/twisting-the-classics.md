## What it is

A method for turning a known game into a new one. Claim: remixes that succeed "keep the classic's core action almost untouched, then change **one** thing about how a session is shaped: how long it lasts, how it ends, what you collect between rounds, or who you play against" [p. 1]. Use: read the case studies once, pick a classic from the Remake Ladder, run the checklist, choose one twist, "prototype it before building anything else" [p. 1].

## 1. Case studies [p. 1-4]

| Game (built on) | Kept | Changed | Added | Why it worked |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Balatro, 2024 (Big Two; a slot-machine roguelike) [p. 1] | 52 cards, poker hands | A score target replaces opponents; chips times mult | Runs of eight antes, a shop between rounds, 150 Jokers | No rules to learn; every run builds a different scoring engine |
| Crossy Road, 2014 (Frogger) [p. 1-2] | Hop across lanes | One endless road; tap to hop | Hundreds of characters, each changing the world | Instant to understand, seconds per run, a reason to return |
| Vampire Survivors, 2022 (Magic Survival) [p. 2] | A hero surrounded by endless monsters | Weapons fire automatically; you only move | An upgrade choice at every level-up, evolutions, 15-30 minute runs, slot-machine chest animations | Almost no input skill, constant choices, a growing screen full of power; very low price |
| Tetris 99, 2019 [p. 2] | The exact rules | 99 players, last board standing | Cleared lines send garbage to chosen opponents | A known game meets the format of the time |
| Pac-Man Championship Edition, 2007 [p. 2-3] | Maze, dots, ghosts, pellets | A five-minute timer replaces lives and levels; speeds up as you score | Clearing half the maze rebuilds the other half | A slow survival game became a fast chase with a clear goal |
| Downwell, 2015 (Spelunky's structure) [p. 3] | A short generated run with shops | You only go down; inputs: left, right, jump | Gunboots: jump in midair fires down, landing reloads | One verb does movement, attack and defence; "plays perfectly on a phone held upright" |
| Super Hexagon, 2012 (a one-day jam game) [p. 3] | Dodge or die | Rotate left or right only; runs last seconds | Spinning screen, pounding soundtrack, levels unlocked by surviving 60 seconds | One decision at high speed, retried hundreds of times |
| Celeste, 2018 (a three-day PICO-8 game) [p. 3-4] | Run, jump, climb | One dash in depth, instant respawns, Assist Mode | A story told through the climb; optional strawberries | A reason to care; difficulty as a choice, not a wall |

Evidence given: Balatro 1 million copies in month one, 5 million by January 2025, one maker; Crossy Road over $10 million and 50 million downloads in three months; Vampire Survivors over 70,000 concurrent Steam players, BAFTA Best Game 2023, about 1,100 pounds of assets; Tetris 99 2.8 million accounts in two months; Downwell Metacritic up to 91 on iOS, its creator's thirteenth small project; Celeste 1.7 million copies [p. 1-4].

The pattern [p. 4]: each kept the core action "recognisable in seconds". What changed was the shape of a session: endless instead of levels, a timer instead of lives, a run with choices between rounds, or a new opponent. "Most also cut controls down rather than adding to them."

## 2. The nine twist techniques [p. 4-6]

"Pick **one** per game" [p. 4].

| | Technique | What and how to apply | Real game | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Change the session shape [p. 4] | Replace levels and lives with an endless run, a timer, or one high-stakes attempt; a 60-second or 3-minute mode with a score to beat | Crossy Road, Pac-Man CE | Endless with no rising challenge bores; ramp speed or density |
| B | Add a run with choices [p. 4-5] | Pick one of a few upgrades between rounds or at level-ups; three cards after each wave; "ten to fifteen upgrades are plenty" | Balatro, Vampire Survivors, Downwell | Upgrades that only change numbers; each should change how you play |
| C | Cut the controls to one smart verb [p. 5] | Remove inputs until one action does several jobs; "what if the player only steers?" | Downwell, Super Hexagon | Cutting until no decision is left |
| D | Automate one part, deepen another [p. 5] | The game does a tiring part so the player focuses on choices; auto-fire | Vampire Survivors | Automating the fun part; keep the decision the player enjoys most |
| E | Change who you play against [p. 5] | Rivals in single-player: a ghost of your best run, simple bots, a daily shared seed | Tetris 99 | Real online multiplayer needs servers |
| F | Combine two classics [p. 5] | One game's action powers another's structure | Balatro, Puzzle Quest | Two rule sets stacked; one should clearly lead |
| G | Flip the player's role [p. 6] | Play the side the original made you fight: the ghosts hunting Pac-Man, the traffic in Frogger | Dungeon Keeper | "The flipped side may have no interesting decisions. Prototype it first." |
| H | Add a theme or a story [p. 6] | A meaning, character or world; a one-screen intro; "a theme where the mechanics make sense" | Celeste, Crossy Road | Long story scenes; let the play carry the story |
| I | Change the platform or the input [p. 6] | Rebuild for another device: one key, mouse-only, touch in a phone browser | Crossy Road, Downwell | Controls that fight the device, like precise jumps on touch |

## 4. Twist checklist [p. 11]

"Write one line per question, then pick the single idea that excites you most."

1. Core: what one action do players repeat? (This stays.)
2. Session: endless, timed, or one-shot?
3. Choices: what could the player pick between rounds?
4. Controls: can one input do two jobs? Can I remove one?
5. Automation: what tiring part could the game do?
6. Rivals: a ghost, a bot, or a daily seed?
7. Mash-up: which other classic could this action power?
8. Flip: what if the player were the enemy, the obstacle, or the world?
9. Meaning: what theme would make these rules feel obvious and fresh?
10. Device: one key, a mouse, or a touch screen?

**Filter.** Keep the idea only if it fits one sentence, keeps the core action recognisable, "adds a decision rather than a rule", and keeps the game in its tier or one tier up at most.

## 5. Testing a twist quickly [p. 11-12]

"Build the classic's core first, then add the twist as the very next feature, before any art or content. If the twist isn't fun with plain shapes, it won't be fun with art."

1. Build the plain core: one screen, shapes only.
2. Add the smallest version of the twist: "three upgrades, not thirty"; for a flip, "one level from the new side".
3. Play 10 rounds yourself: a new decision every round, or just more to track?
4. Silent test with one person: tell them only the controls. Do they understand the twist unaided, and do they play again?
5. Decide: keep, simplify, or swap to the second-favourite idea. "Two or three tweaks at most before switching."

**Works:** players talk about the twist itself ("I got the triple-ball upgrade!"), runs feel different from each other, testers replay without being asked.
**Does not:** you have to explain it, it needs a tutorial, or the game is more fun with it turned off.

## What it means for Vampire: No Survivors (all inference)

- The survivor-like base already carries D and B. "You are the vampire hunting humans" is G plus H; it counts as a twist only if it adds a decision or reshapes the session, otherwise it is a re-skin.
- G's trap applies directly: the hunter's side needs its own decisions. "One level from the new side" is what the plain-shapes prototype is.
- Give each variation's twist an off-switch, so "more fun with it turned off" can be checked.
- The owner is the one silent tester: give only the controls. Signs to watch: the owner names the twist unprompted and replays unasked.
- The ten rounds of step 3 can only be played here by bots or a lab harness: proxy, to be reported as proxy.
- The document has nothing on phone layout, performance, audio, balance numbers or market size.
