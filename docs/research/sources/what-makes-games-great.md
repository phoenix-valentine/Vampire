## What it is

A short design guide for small browser games [p. 1]. Thesis: great games make players feel themselves getting
better, give them choices that matter, and answer every action with instant, satisfying feedback; everything
else supports those three [p. 1]. Each principle has four parts (What, Why, Example, Apply); psychology claims
are labelled solid, mixed or myth [p. 1]. Principles are "defaults, not laws": each has been broken by a
successful game [p. 1]. It ends with a ranked Top 10 and a checklist to use on every game [p. 1]. No AI
prompts, no code, nothing on phones, performance, market or release.

## Frameworks, lists and checklists

### F1. Why brains enjoy games: seven principles

Framing: people play because games reliably make them feel capable, free to choose, and constantly learning;
brain chemistry matters less than most articles claim [p. 1].

1. **Competence, autonomy, relatedness (solid)** [p. 1]. Self-Determination Theory. In four studies by Ryan,
   Rigby and Przybylski, autonomy and competence predicted enjoyment and future play; intuitive controls
   boosted the feeling of competence. Example: Tetris, you see yourself get faster and cleaner.
   Apply: make improvement visible (a best score, a faster time), give at least one real choice, make controls
   easy to grasp in seconds. A shareable score is the single-player nod to relatedness.
2. **Learning is the fun (design theory, widely accepted)** [p. 2]. Koster: fun is the brain learning
   patterns; with nothing left to learn, boredom sets in (tic-tac-toe dies after a few games, chess never
   does). Apply: every minute of play offers something new to learn, or a harder version of something known.
   When the game stops teaching it is over, so end it or add a twist.
3. **Dopamine is about wanting and surprise, not pleasure (solid, often misreported)** [p. 2]. Dopamine
   signals "better than expected" and drives wanting, separate from liking; the two can split, which is how
   compulsion without enjoyment happens. A rare unexpected drop thrills; the same reward every time stops
   registering. Apply: mix reliable rewards with occasional surprises, let players see good things coming,
   and check that players like playing, not just that they keep clicking.
4. **Challenge that matches skill (solid, with nuance)** [p. 2]. Flow needs challenge roughly matching skill
   (too hard: anxiety; too easy: boredom). Two twists: an easier mode raised competence and enjoyment;
   difficulty that rose and fell beat constant balance for flow and enjoyment. Example: Tetris speeds up, but
   each new level starts with a breather. Apply: start easier than you think, then ramp up in waves of push
   and rest, not a straight line.
5. **Visible progress pulls people forward (solid)** [p. 2-3]. Goal-gradient effect: people speed up near a
   goal. A 12-stamp card with 2 free stamps was finished faster than a blank 10-stamp card; right after the
   reward, activity dropped and people were most likely to leave. Examples: XP bars, level-progress meters,
   "2 more to unlock". Apply: show progress toward the next goal at all times, start players with a little
   progress already made, reveal the next goal the moment one is reached.
6. **Curiosity is its own reward (solid)** [p. 3]. Wanting to know what comes next motivates by itself
   (Gruber and colleagues, 2014). Examples: a locked character as a silhouette, a door you can see but can't
   reach. Apply: hint at the unseen: a "?" unlock, a new enemy glimpsed in the distance, a score tier with a
   mysterious name.
7. **Rewards can backfire (mixed: experts disagree)** [p. 3]. A meta-analysis of 128 studies found tangible
   rewards undermined intrinsic motivation; critics found the opposite, and rewards for low-interest tasks
   can raise motivation. Pay for chores feels like a job; rewards that open new ways to play feel like a
   gift. Apply: rewards should open up more play (new options, new challenges), not pay for something boring.

### F2. Myths to ignore [p. 3-4]

| Myth | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| Dopamine is the pleasure chemical | It mainly drives wanting and learning from surprise; pleasure (liking) runs on separate systems [p. 3] |
| Games release as much dopamine as drugs | The 1998 brain-scan study found real dopamine release, but players were paid for each level [p. 4] |
| Perfect difficulty balance is best | Rising and falling challenge did better than constant balance [p. 4] |
| Random rewards are the secret to great games | Powerful, but paid random rewards are linked to problem gambling. Great games need a fun core first [p. 4] |

### F3. Core building blocks: eight, "build these in roughly this order" [p. 4]

Definition: a great small game is a fun action, wrapped in instant feedback, with a fair challenge and a
reason to try once more [p. 4].

1. **A core loop that's fun on its own** [p. 4]. The action repeated every few seconds (jump, shoot, match,
   dodge). If the core isn't fun, no amount of dressing will fix it; art and sound only amplify what is there.
   Example: Vampire Survivors is mostly moving and surviving, and that loop carries the whole game.
   Apply: describe the loop in one sentence and make it fun with plain shapes first. Then three layers: the
   action (seconds), a round or wave (a minute), a reason to play again (a best score or an unlock).
2. **Game feel and feedback** [p. 4-5]. Instant response plus visual and sound reactions to every action
   ("juice"). Feedback confirms competence instantly, so the player feels skilled before achieving anything.
   Example: Celeste forgives near-misses (jump just after leaving a ledge; a jump pressed just before landing
   still counts). Apply: for every player action, decide what the player sees and hears. Add small forgiveness
   where precision is needed. Never let effects hide what's happening.
3. **Fair failure and fast retry** [p. 5]. The player understands why they lost and is playing again within
   seconds; unclear deaths feel like the game cheated. Apply: one key restarts in under 2 seconds. Show what
   killed the player. After a loss, a tester should be able to say what they'd do differently.
4. **Challenge that teaches** [p. 5]. Starts gentle, one idea at a time, rises in waves (Super Mario Bros.
   World 1-1: first enemy on open ground). Apply: introduce each new thing alone, let the player practise it
   safely, test it, then combine it with something they know. Playing within 10 seconds, no text walls.
5. **Interesting decisions** [p. 5-6]. Sid Meier: a decision is interesting when there's no obvious right
   answer and the outcome matters; obvious choices are chores (Spelunky: treasure versus safety).
   Apply: for each choice, write down why a smart player might pick each option; if you can't, cut it. Add one
   optional risky path with a bigger reward.
6. **Progression and rewards** [p. 6]. The player gains skill, new options or new goals (Balatro hides the
   score preview, so playing a hand is a moment of suspense). Apply: prefer unlocks that change how you play
   over bigger numbers. Always show the next goal. Let big moments build up with sound and spectacle before
   the payoff.
7. **Variety and replayability** [p. 6]. Each round poses a slightly different problem, not just different
   scenery (Spelunky: hand-made room templates with random changes, always new but never broken).
   Apply: design a handful of pieces by hand (enemy waves, layouts, upgrades) and let the game shuffle them.
   Randomise what the player is dealt, then let skill decide the outcome. Make parts that are mediocre alone
   but strong together.
8. **Theme that explains the rules** [p. 6]. A setting where players guess how things work from how they look
   (Plants vs. Zombies: a sunflower makes sun, a wall-nut blocks). Apply: if you have to explain a rule in
   text, try reskinning it so it explains itself.

### F4. Why players stay or quit [p. 7]

| Why players stay | Example | Why players quit |
|---|---|---|
| Simple rules with endless depth | Tetris and chess | Nothing left to learn once the trick is found |
| Every session is a new problem | Spelunky and Balatro | Every round plays out the same |
| Room for self-expression | Minecraft builds, Balatro combos | Only one correct way to play |
| Moments worth sharing | Wordle's results grid | Nothing to talk about afterwards |
| Respect for the player's time | Wordle: about three minutes a day | Grinding, nagging, punishment for taking a break |

Two more reasons players drift away: unfair or confusing failure, and the slump right after a big reward with
no new goal in sight [p. 7]. Target for a small game: a 5-15 minute experience that ends with "one more try",
plus a reason to return: beating your best score, an unlock you glimpsed, or a new shuffle of the pieces [p. 7].

### F5. Compelling vs. manipulative [p. 7-8]

The test: "looking back, would players thank you for the time they spent?" [p. 7]. Manipulative techniques
come in three kinds: time-based, money-based and social pressure (grinding, pay-to-skip, pressure to recruit
friends) [p. 7]. Counter-view: no pattern is dark in itself; how and why it is used matters [p. 8].

| Technique | Healthy version | Manipulative version |
|---|---|---|
| Random rewards | Earned through play, and they change how you play | Bought with real money, with hidden odds [p. 7] |
| Daily play | A daily puzzle you can do any time | Streaks that reset if you miss a day [p. 7] |
| Waiting | Natural stopping points | Timers designed to annoy, then sold as skips [p. 8] |
| Progress | Paced by skill and play | Deliberately slowed to sell boosts [p. 8] |
| Endings | A satisfying end or a natural pause | Endless loops with no stopping cue [p. 8] |

Apply: no fake urgency, no punishing breaks, a clear place to stop. Hard to put down because one more round
sounds fun, not because stopping feels costly [p. 8].

### F6. The 10 most important principles, ranked, each with its test [p. 8]

Ranked by how much each decides whether a small game is good. The first three are non-negotiable; the rest
multiply them. Most great small games excel at 2 or 3 and are solid at the rest, so pick the ones your game
will be great at [p. 8]. Principle text is the document's. The "Test" lines use the document's wording from
the checklist and Apply lines; pairing them to principles is my arrangement (inference).

1. **A core loop that's fun with plain shapes.** If the 30-second experience isn't fun, fix it before
   anything else. Test: loop stated in one sentence; fun with plain shapes [p. 9].
2. **Instant, satisfying feedback.** Every action responds immediately with something to see and hear.
   Test: every player action has an instant visual and sound response; effects never hide the action [p. 9].
3. **The player feels themselves getting better.** Improvement is visible and earned. Test: no dedicated
   checklist line; nearest is visible improvement such as a best score or faster time [p. 1].
4. **Fair challenge that rises in waves.** Start easy, push, rest, push harder, with fast retries and clear
   causes of failure. Test: waves, not a flat line or a wall; testers can say what they'd do differently;
   restart under 2 seconds; the game shows what killed the player [p. 5, 9].
5. **Interesting decisions.** Choices with real trade-offs and no obvious right answer. Test: at least one
   choice has a real trade-off; a written reason to pick each option, else cut [p. 6, 9].
6. **Something new to learn every minute.** New situations, combinations or twists, before boredom sets in.
   Test: each round feels a little different from the last [p. 9].
7. **Always a next goal.** Visible progress, and a new goal the moment one is reached. Test: there is always
   a visible next goal [p. 9].
8. **Teach through play.** Playing within 10 seconds; one new idea at a time. Test: playing within 10 seconds
   and goal understood within a minute; easy opening; no text walls [p. 5, 9].
9. **Rewards that open up more play.** New options over bigger numbers, with occasional surprises.
   Test: rewards change how the game plays, not just the numbers [p. 9].
10. **Respect for the player.** A natural place to stop, and no tricks to keep them clicking. Test: natural
    stopping point; nothing punishes a break; testers played again without being asked [p. 9].

## What it means for Vampire: No Survivors

### (a) Process for an AI building alone

- Build in the document's order: loop, feel, fair failure and retry, teaching challenge, decisions,
  progression, variety, theme [p. 4]. Theme is last, which suits a plain-shapes prototype (inference).
- Run the 16-item checklist at the end of the prototype and again before the owner's test [p. 9].
- Pick the 2 or 3 principles the game will be great at [p. 8].
- Write two artefacts the document asks for: what the player sees and hears for every action [p. 5], and why
  a smart player might pick each option of each choice [p. 6].
- Three checklist lines need humans: "fun with plain shapes", "testers can say what they'd do differently",
  "testers played again without being asked" [p. 9]. Inference: report them as "could not check" and make
  them the questions for the owner's prototype test.
- Inference: if three variations are built, judge each on whether its 30-second experience is fun [p. 8].

### (b) Core loop, controls and feel

- The document names Vampire Survivors as "mostly moving and surviving" [p. 4]. Inference: movement and
  survival must feel good before weapons, art or meta are added.
- State the loop in one sentence and define the three layers for this game [p. 4].
- Every action gets an instant visual and sound response [p. 5, 9]. Inference: the checklist is run at the
  end of the prototype and includes sound, so the plain-shapes prototype needs sound. Inference: with
  auto-attack, cover hit, kill, pickup, level-up, damage taken and death.
- Small forgiveness where precision is needed [p. 5]. Inference: hitbox smaller than the drawn shape,
  generous pickup radius, short grace period after a hit.
- Effects must never hide what's happening [p. 5, 9]. Inference: the main feel risk with many enemies and
  projectiles on a small screen.
- Controls graspable in seconds; intuitive controls raise felt competence [p. 1].

### (c) Content, difficulty, builds and balance

- Start easier than you think; waves of push and rest, not a straight line [p. 2, 8].
- Each new enemy or mechanic: alone, practised safely, tested, then combined [p. 5]; something new or a
  harder version of something known every minute [p. 2].
- Upgrade choices need no obvious right answer; cut options nobody smart would pick; one optional risky path
  with a bigger reward [p. 5-6].
- Prefer upgrades that change how you play over bigger numbers [p. 6]; parts mediocre alone but strong
  together [p. 6]; more than one correct way to play [p. 7].
- Hand-make a handful of enemy waves, layouts and upgrades and shuffle them; randomise the deal, let skill
  decide the outcome [p. 6].
- Next goal always visible, a little progress pre-made, the next goal revealed the moment one is reached
  [p. 3]. Inference: an XP bar that starts partly filled and no dead moment after a level-up or boss.
- Reliable rewards plus occasional surprises [p. 2]; build-up with sound and spectacle before big payoffs [p. 6].
- Curiosity hooks: a "?" unlock, a new enemy glimpsed in the distance, a mysteriously named score tier [p. 3].
- A 5-15 minute experience ending with "one more try" [p. 7], with a satisfying end or natural pause, not an
  endless loop with no stopping cue [p. 8].
- Theme should explain rules without text [p. 6]. Inference: use vampire conventions (blood as health,
  daylight as danger).

### (d) Phone: layout, input, performance, audio

The document says nothing about phones. Related lines only: controls graspable in seconds [p. 1], one-key
restart in under 2 seconds [p. 5], a sound response to every action [p. 9]. Inference: on a phone "one key"
becomes one tap within thumb reach.

### (e) Testing and playtests

- Use F6 and F7. Inference: playing within 10 seconds, restart under 2 seconds, visible next goal and cause
  of death shown can be checked by an automated run; the rest need a person.
- Check that players like playing, not just that they keep clicking [p. 2].
- Ask after a loss what the tester would do differently; note whether they replay unasked [p. 9].
- Overall: would players thank you for the time they spent? [p. 7]

### (f) Market, positioning, name, release

Almost nothing. A shareable score serves relatedness [p. 1] and "moments worth sharing" keeps players [p. 7].
If money is ever added: no paid random rewards with hidden odds, no reset streaks, no timers sold as skips,
no progress slowed to sell boosts [p. 7-8].

## Warnings and anti-patterns

- Dressing up an unfun core; art and sound only amplify what is there [p. 4].
- Effects that hide what's happening [p. 5, 9].
- Unclear, unfair or confusing deaths [p. 5, 7].
- Text walls, and rules that need text [p. 5, 6].
- Starting too hard; a straight-line ramp, a flat line, a wall, or constant "perfect" balance [p. 2, 4, 9].
- Obvious choices, which are chores [p. 5].
- Rewards that are only bigger numbers or that pay for boring tasks [p. 3, 6].
- The same reward every time [p. 2].
- The slump after a big reward with no new goal in sight [p. 3, 7].
- Rounds that play the same, variety that is only scenery, one correct way to play, nothing left to learn
  once the trick is found [p. 6, 7].
- Compulsion without enjoyment: clicking is not liking [p. 2].
- Grinding, nagging, punished breaks, fake urgency, reset streaks, endless loops with no stopping cue [p. 7-8].
- The four myths in F2 [p. 3-4].
- Treating the principles as laws, not defaults [p. 1].
